01Music — 99LETTERS

It started with sound.

Before I made images, I made records. As 99LETTERS I have released music on labels in London and beyond — Disciples, Phantom Limb, Seagrave.

On each release I worked as art director alongside the label's designers. Making music taught me the habit I now use for everything: sample, cut down, repeat, and listen for the moment it starts to breathe.

02The studio

A small room by Lake Biwa.

Kinoshita Studio is one person and one room in Shiga, Japan. The music and the images are made at the same desk.

I write the tools, make the images, set the type and build the pages myself — so the idea never has to be translated twice.

Film is shot, scanned and printed here. Proofs go straight onto the wall.

03Why I make graphics

Japan, without the “Japanese” look.

Abroad, Japan is usually drawn with the same few things: a brush stroke, vermilion red, a serif Mincho face. I wanted to see what is left when you take all of that away and keep only the letters.

So I treat Japanese characters the way I treat a sound. I sample them, lower their resolution, give each piece a texture and let them move. What comes out does not look traditional — but you can still feel that it could only come from these letters.

I don’t start with a purpose. I start by building a small instrument, play it, and let the accidents arrive. The judgment comes afterwards.

Brush, scanner glass, a home printer, film — the tools change, the habit doesn’t.

04How the grain is made — TSUBU

A letter, played like a synth.

TSUBU (粒, “grain”) is one of the browser tools I wrote for myself. It turns a Japanese character into a field of 8-bit cells, then lets me play those cells like oscillators.

It is a tool, not a filter: every image is a single performance, recorded at the moment it settles.

Step 1

Start from a letter.

One Japanese character, typed or drawn. Nothing else goes in — no photographs.

Step 2

Lower the resolution.

The letter drops into a coarse grid, like sound sampled at a low bit-rate. The shape survives; the detail is gone.

Step 3

Give every cell a voice.

Each cell becomes a small tile of light — a stripe, a shine, a direction. Together they behave like a chord.

Step 4

Play, then stop.

I turn the dials and let it move, then keep the one take where nothing wavers. Stills, loops and prints all come from that take.

TSUBU is open in the browser: kinoshita.studio/tsubu

05How I decide

Three rules I keep.

  1. 01

    One instrument per piece.

    If a work needs two tools to explain it, it is two works.

  2. 02

    Letters only.

    No photographs, no borrowed ornament. The way it was made should be enough to recognise it.

  3. 03

    Keep the take that holds still.

    The clever take is usually the wrong one. I keep the one that would still feel right on a wall a year later.

06Work — by instrument

Letters broken into 8-bit grain and played as light. Motion loops and stills.

MASU / grid

枡

A square measure, filled with chrome. Each cell is chosen by hand from a small palette of shapes.

Light of letters

字の光

One character set inside a frame of lines, until the frame and the letter become one surface.

NIJIMI / OBORO — bleed & haze

滲・朧

Letters that soak and smear, and images stretched until only their weather is left.

07Cover art

The same instruments, on a sleeve.

A set of sleeves made with the tools above — grain, print, stretch and paper. Each one starts from a single shape and is finished as a full cover, ready for vinyl, cassette or a streaming thumbnail.

Self-initiated sleeves for imagined releases. The artists and titles are fictional.
